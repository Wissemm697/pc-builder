Before you start

How the parts work together

A computer is a team of specialised parts. To see who does what, picture a restaurant kitchen: every part has its own job, and none of them is any use without the others.

  • The head chef

    Processor (CPU)

    It reads the recipes (the programs' instructions) and does the work, billions of times a second. An 8-core processor is like 8 chefs cooking at the same time.
  • The worktop

    Memory (RAM)

    Whatever ingredients are in use sit here, within the chef's reach. It's very fast, but everything gets cleared away at closing time: RAM empties as soon as the PC is switched off.
  • The fridge and the pantry

    Storage (SSD)

    Everything is kept here: the operating system, your programs, your files. Nothing is lost when the power goes off, but fetching something from here takes much longer than grabbing it from the worktop.
  • The kitchen itself

    Motherboard

    The walls, the corridors, the power sockets: it connects every part, gives each one its power and carries information between them.
  • 1,000 kitchen assistants at once

    Graphics card (GPU)

    Each assistant does one simple task (working out the colour of a single dot on the screen, say), but there are thousands of them working at the same time. Unbeatable for images, and for artificial intelligence too.
  • The power supply to the building

    Power supply (PSU)

    It turns the current from the wall socket (230 V AC in Europe, 120 V in North America) into the low-voltage DC the components need (mostly 12 V), and cuts everything off if something goes wrong.
  • The building and the extractor fan

    Case and fans

    The case protects and organises everything. Like a kitchen extractor fan, the fans bring in fresh air and push out the heat the parts give off.

A program's journey, from storage to screen

Say you launch a game. Here's what happens, in order.

  1. 1

    Storage

    The game's files are read from the SSD (the fridge). This is the slowest step: it's what loading screens are made of.

  2. 2

    RAM

    They're copied into RAM (the worktop), where the processor can reach them in a few tens of nanoseconds.

  3. 3

    CPU

    The processor (the chef) runs the game's logic: movement, physics, the characters' behaviour, sound… and draws up a list of what needs to be on screen.

  4. 4

    GPU

    The graphics card (the kitchen brigade) works out the millions of dots (pixels) in each frame, all in parallel, using its own fast memory: the VRAM.

  5. 5

    Screen

    The image travels down the cable (DisplayPort or HDMI) to the monitor, which shows 60 to 165 of them per second depending on its refresh rate.

In between all these parts, the motherboard acts as the road network: data travels along "motorways" called buses, the most important of which is PCIe.

Part 1 of 8 · The head chef

The processor (CPU)

An AMD AM5 processor (40 × 40 mm). Flip it over to see its ~1,700 gold pads.

What it does

The processor (CPU, short for "central processing unit") carries out the programs' instructions: additions, comparisons, moving data around… Everything the computer does goes through it at some point.

Physically, it's a silicon chip (the die) soldered onto a small green board (the substrate) and covered by a metal lid, the IHS, which spreads the heat out towards the cooler. Underneath, gold pads press against the pins of the socket, the motherboard's processor mount. On modern LGA sockets (AMD AM5, Intel LGA 1851), the pins are on the motherboard, not on the processor.

Think of it this way The processor is the head chef: it follows recipes step by step, very quickly. Each core is an independent chef, so 8 cores means 8 chefs working on different dishes at the same time.

How to read the spec sheet

Typical specs: a mainstream mid-range processor

Cores / threads
8 / 16
A core is a complete processing unit. A thread is a sequence of instructions to run; thanks to SMT, an AMD core switches between 2 threads so it's never left idle. Intel's Core Ultra 200 series mixes "P" (performance) and "E" (efficient) cores, without SMT. 6 to 8 cores is plenty for gaming, coding or data work.
Clock speed
3.8 GHz base · 5.4 GHz boost
The number of cycles per second: 5 GHz means 5 billion. The "boost" speed is reached as long as temperature and power draw allow it. Only compare GHz between processors of the same generation: a newer design gets more work done in each cycle.
L3 cache
32 MB
The cache is a small, extremely fast memory built into the processor: the chef's own spice rack, right at hand. The more there is, the less time the processor spends waiting on RAM. AMD's "X3D" models have far more of it (96 MB), which mainly helps in games.
Socket
AM5 (LGA 1718)
The type of mount. A processor only fits a motherboard with the same socket. Right now that's AM5 for AMD and LGA 1851 for Intel (Intel's next generation, expected in early 2027, moves to LGA 1954).
TDP
65 W
The TDP tells you roughly how much heat has to be removed, so it's what you use to choose the cooling. Mainstream processors usually sit between 65 and 125 W.
Memory / integrated graphics
DDR5 · iGPU yes
Which type of RAM it takes (DDR5 only on AM5), and whether it has a small built-in graphics unit (iGPU), handy for troubleshooting without a graphics card. Watch out: Intel processors whose name ends in "F" don't have one.

A sense of scale

  • 0.2 ns That's one cycle at 5 GHz. In that time, light travels just 6 cm.
  • Billions of transistors fit on a chip the size of a fingernail.
  • 95 °C At around 95 to 105 °C, depending on the model, the processor protects itself by slowing down (throttling). That's why the cooler matters.

Compatibility

  • With the motherboard: the same socket (AM5 ↔ AM5). Also check the board maker's list of supported processors: a processor newer than the board may need a BIOS update.
  • With the RAM: the memory type (DDR5 on AM5).
  • With the cooler: the mounting kit must support your socket (AM5 reuses AM4 mounts), and it must be able to handle the TDP.

Beginner mistakes

  • Forcing it. The processor should drop flat into the socket on its own. If it resists, it's the wrong way round: line up the two gold triangles.
  • Touching the socket pins. They're very thin and bend at the slightest touch, and a bent pin can make the board unusable.
  • Throwing away the socket's plastic cover. It pops off when you close the lever. Keep it: manufacturers often ask for it if you send the board back.
  • Comparing GHz between two brands or two generations. It tells you nothing: look at benchmarks instead.

Part 2 of 8 · The kitchen that connects everything

The motherboard

An ATX motherboard (30.5 × 24.4 cm). Hover over or tap the labels to identify each area.

What it does

The motherboard is a large printed circuit board (PCB) that everything plugs into. It delivers power to each part, moves data between them, and holds the BIOS/UEFI, the small piece of software that starts the PC.

Its main areas: the processor socket and its VRM (the stage that produces the processor's voltage, under the finned heatsinks), the 4 RAM slots, the PCIe x16 slot for the graphics card, the M.2 slots for SSDs, the chipset, the 24-pin and 8-pin EPS power connectors, the headers (for the case's front panel, fans and USB) and the rear ports (I/O).

Think of it this way The motherboard is the kitchen itself: the worktops, the passages between stations, the power sockets. A badly designed kitchen slows the whole brigade down, however good the chef.

How to read the spec sheet

Typical specs: a mid-range ATX motherboard

Form factor
ATX (30.5 × 24.4 cm)
The board's size and the position of its 9 mounting holes. The case must support this size. ATX is the standard size for tower PCs.
Socket / chipset
AM5 · B-series chipset
The socket decides which processors fit. The chipset runs some of the ports (USB, SATA, secondary M.2) and sets the feature list. The "B" range (B650/B850 for AMD, B860 for Intel, for example) is the mid-range: everything you need, nothing you don't.
Memory
4 × DDR5 (dual channel)
The type and number of slots. With 2 sticks, you use slots A2 and B2 to get dual channel.
Storage
2 × M.2 NVMe · 4 × SATA
The slots for M.2 SSDs (and their PCIe generation), plus SATA ports for older drives. The M.2 slot closest to the processor is usually the fastest.
Expansion
1 × PCIe x16 (reinforced) + secondary slots
The main x16 slot, with a metal reinforcement, takes the graphics card. "x16" means 16 data lanes.
Connectivity
USB-A/C, 2.5 Gb/s Ethernet, Wi-Fi, audio
The ports on the back and the internal headers (front USB-C, fans…). The motherboard's video outputs only work with the processor's integrated graphics.

A sense of scale

  • ~32 GB/s The bandwidth of a PCIe 4.0 x16 slot in each direction: the graphics card's motorway.
  • 6 to 8 layers of copper traces are stacked inside the 1.6 mm thickness of a modern board.
  • ~1 V The VRM turns the power supply's 12 V into roughly 1 to 1.4 V for the processor, which can then draw several tens of amps.

Compatibility

  • Processor: the same socket, and a compatible BIOS version (see the maker's "CPU support" list). Many boards have a BIOS Flashback button to update without a processor installed.
  • RAM: the same generation (DDR5).
  • Case: the form factor (ATX) must be on the list of supported sizes.
  • Power supply: it must provide the 24-pin and the 8-pin EPS (sometimes a second 4/8-pin, optional on mid-range builds).

Beginner mistakes

  • Forgetting the I/O shield when it isn't built into the board: it clips into the case before the motherboard goes in.
  • Leaving a standoff with no hole above it: it touches the back of the board and can cause a short circuit.
  • Forgetting the 8-pin EPS cable in the top left: the PC won't start.
  • Overtightening the screws: snug, then a small quarter turn, is enough.

Part 3 of 8 · The worktop

Memory (RAM)

A 16 GB DDR5 stick (133 × 31 mm): 8 memory chips, a voltage regulator (PMIC) and 288 contacts.

What it does

Memory (RAM) keeps the data and programs you're currently using close at hand. It responds hundreds of times faster than an SSD, but it's volatile: everything in it disappears the moment the power is cut. That's why you "save" a file: you're copying it from RAM to storage.

The typical setup in 2026: 2 sticks of 16 GB of DDR5, so 32 GB in total, fitted in slots A2 and B2 to run in dual channel (the two sticks work in parallel, which doubles the bandwidth).

Think of it this way RAM is the worktop: the bigger it is, the more dishes the chef can prepare at once without running back and forth to the fridge. But at the end of the night, everything gets cleared away.

How to read the spec sheet

Typical specs: a 32 GB (2 × 16 GB) DDR5-6000 CL30 kit

Capacity
32 GB (2 × 16 GB)
The size of the worktop. 16 GB is a comfortable minimum; 32 GB is the safe choice for gaming, coding, data work or video editing. Buy a kit of 2 identical sticks.
Generation
DDR5
DDR5 and DDR4 aren't interchangeable: the notch at the bottom is in a different place, so one can't go into the other's slot (and forcing it is a bad idea).
Speed
6000 MT/s
The number of transfers per second (MT/s, often wrongly written as "MHz"). Out of the box, RAM starts at a slower standard speed: you have to turn on the EXPO/XMP profile in the BIOS. On AM5, 6000 MT/s is the usual sweet spot.
Latency
CL30
The number of cycles you wait before the data arrives (CAS latency). Lower is better, but you have to factor in the speed: real latency ≈ CL × 2000 ÷ speed = 30 × 2000 ÷ 6000 = 10 ns.
Voltage / profile
1.35 V · EXPO (AMD) / XMP (Intel)
The voltage needed to hold the rated speed, applied automatically by the profile. AMD calls it EXPO, Intel calls it XMP, and many kits support both.

A sense of scale

  • ~1,000× RAM answers in about 80 ns, an NVMe SSD in 50 to 100 µs. In access time (latency), RAM is roughly 1,000 times faster. If a trip to RAM took 1 second, a trip to the SSD would take a quarter of an hour, and a hard drive more than a day.
  • ~14× In throughput (amount of data per second), the gap is smaller: two DDR5-6000 sticks in dual channel reach ~96 GB/s in theory, against ~7 GB/s for a very good PCIe 4.0 SSD.

Compatibility

  • Motherboard: the same generation (DDR5). The maker's QVL lists the kits it has tested.
  • Slots: with 2 sticks, A2 and B2 (the 2nd and 4th slots counting from the processor). The manual says so, and it's often printed on the board.
  • CPU cooler: very tall sticks (with big heatsinks) can get in the way of the cooler's fan.

Beginner mistakes

  • Not pushing it in until it clicks. You have to press hard, on both ends: a stick that isn't fully seated stops the PC from starting.
  • Putting the sticks side by side (A1 + A2): you lose dual channel.
  • Forgetting EXPO/XMP: your RAM then runs slower than what you paid for.
  • Panicking on the first boot. With DDR5, the board "trains" the memory (memory training), so the first start can take several minutes on a black screen. That's normal: don't switch it off.

Part 4 of 8 · The fridge and the pantry

Storage (M.2 NVMe SSD)

A 1 TB M.2 2280 SSD: 22 × 80 mm, barely bigger than a stick of gum… and no cables at all.

What it does

Storage keeps everything, even with the power off: the operating system, your programs, your files. An SSD uses flash memory (NAND) and has no moving parts. The M.2 2280 format screws straight onto the motherboard, and the NVMe protocol lets it talk to the processor over PCIe lanes, which is why it's so fast.

The chip on the left is the controller (the SSD's brain). It spreads the data across the flash memory chips hidden under the label.

Think of it this way Storage is the fridge and the pantry: everything is kept there, even overnight, but it takes time to go and fetch the ingredients. An NVMe SSD is a fridge right next to the kitchen; a hard drive is a storeroom at the far end of the yard.

How to read the spec sheet

Typical specs: a 1 TB M.2 2280 NVMe SSD

Form factor
M.2 2280 (M key)
22 mm wide and 80 mm long: by far the most common size. The notch in the contacts (the "M key") matches the M.2 slots meant for SSDs.
Interface
PCIe 4.0 x4, NVMe
4 fourth-generation PCIe lanes, so about 8 GB/s at the theoretical maximum. PCIe 5.0 SSDs double that, but run hotter for a gain you'll barely notice in everyday use.
Capacity
1 TB
1 TB = 1,000 GB. Windows and a few recent games (often 50 to 150 GB each) fill it quickly: 1 TB is a good start, 2 TB if you play a lot.
Sequential speeds
7,000 / 6,000 MB/s
Reading / writing large files, under ideal conditions. Day to day, how snappy it feels depends mostly on small random accesses (IOPS) and latency, where every NVMe SSD is already excellent.
Endurance
600 TBW
The total amount of data you can write before it wears out (TBW). 600 TB is 160 GB written every day for 10 years: more than enough.

A sense of scale

  • 3 s Copying a 20 GB file takes about 3 s on a PCIe 4.0 NVMe SSD, 40 s on a SATA SSD and 2 minutes on a hard drive (under ideal conditions).

Other kinds of storage (as extras)

Compatibility

  • Motherboard: an M-key M.2 slot that supports NVMe, of the same or a newer PCIe generation (otherwise the SSD still works, just more slowly).
  • Shared lanes: on some boards, using an M.2 slot disables SATA ports or slows down a PCIe slot. The manual will tell you.
  • Length: the slot must take 80 mm drives (it almost always does).

Beginner mistakes

  • Forgetting the screw (or the latch): the SSD stays tilted up at 30° and eventually loses contact.
  • Leaving the plastic film on the thermal pad of the motherboard's M.2 heatsink.
  • Installing Windows on the wrong drive: during setup, make sure you pick the NVMe SSD.

Part 5 of 8 · A brigade of 1,000 assistants

The graphics card (GPU)

A mid-range graphics card: 28 cm long, 2 fans, one 8-pin power socket.

What it does

The graphics card draws the images you see on screen. Its chip, the GPU, contains thousands of small processing units working in parallel: perfect for handling millions of pixels, and also for artificial intelligence and scientific computing (which is why it's used in data science). It has its own ultra-fast memory, the VRAM, and its own cooling system: a heatsink, heat pipes and fans.

Think of it this way The CPU is a brilliant chef who can cook anything; the GPU is a brigade of 1,000 kitchen assistants who can only do simple jobs… but all at the same time. To peel 10,000 potatoes, the brigade wins hands down.

How to read the spec sheet

Typical specs: a mid-range graphics card

VRAM
8 to 16 GB (GDDR6/GDDR7)
The card's memory, where game textures are stored. 8 GB is getting tight at 1440p with recent games; 12 to 16 GB gives you more headroom.
Power draw
~150 to 220 W
How much power the card uses (often listed as TGP or TBP). It decides what power supply you need: the maker gives a recommended PSU wattage (often 550 to 650 W at this level).
Dimensions
28 cm · 2.5 slots
Compare the length with the case's "maximum GPU length". The thickness in "slots" tells you how many expansion slots it takes up at the back (here, it also covers the second M.2 slot).
Power connector
1 × 8-pin PCIe
The PCIe slot supplies up to 75 W, and an 8-pin connector up to 150 W more. Some more powerful cards use a 16-pin connector called 12V-2x6.
Video outputs
3 × DisplayPort · 1 × HDMI
For connecting one or more screens. DisplayPort is the standard for PC monitors, HDMI for TVs (see the Peripherals section).
Interface
PCIe x16 (4.0 or 5.0)
PCIe generations work with each other: a PCIe 5.0 card runs in a 4.0 slot, usually with very little performance loss for a card at this level.

A sense of scale

  • 600 million A 1440p frame has 3.7 million pixels. At 165 frames per second, the card works out more than 600 million pixels every second.
  • Thousands of processing units in the GPU, against 6 to 8 (far more versatile) cores in the processor.
  • 3 to 5× VRAM delivers 300 to more than 500 GB/s: 3 to 5 times the bandwidth of the PC's main RAM.

Compatibility

  • Case: card length < maximum supported length (fans or a radiator at the front can eat into that space).
  • Power supply: enough wattage and the right connector (8-pin PCIe or 12V-2x6).
  • Motherboard: a free PCIe x16 slot (the top, reinforced one).
  • Monitor: an input that matches the card's outputs (DisplayPort or HDMI).

Beginner mistakes

  • Plugging the monitor into the motherboard. Mistake number one: the motherboard's outputs only work with integrated graphics. With a graphics card, the monitor plugs into the graphics card.
  • Forgetting the PCIe power cable, or not pushing it in until it clicks.
  • Using a "daisy-chained" cable (two plugs on the same cable) for a card with two connectors: use two separate cables instead.
  • Not screwing the bracket to the case: the card wobbles and puts strain on the slot.

Part 6 of 8 · The power coming in

The power supply (PSU)

A 750 W, 80 PLUS Gold, semi-modular ATX power supply (15 × 8.6 × 14 cm).

What it does

The power supply (PSU) turns the alternating current from the wall (230 V in Europe, 120 V in North America) into low-voltage direct current: mostly 12 V (for the processor and the graphics card), plus some 5 V and 3.3 V. A good PSU delivers steady power and has built-in protections that cut everything off if there's a short circuit or an overload: it's what keeps every other part safe.

This one is semi-modular: the essential cables (the motherboard's 24-pin and the processor's 8-pin EPS) are fixed, and the others plug in as needed.

Think of it this way The power supply is the kitchen's mains connection and fuse box: it converts the power from the grid for each appliance and cuts everything off if something goes wrong.

How to read the spec sheet

Typical specs: a 750 W ATX power supply

Wattage
750 W
The most it can deliver continuously. A mid-range build draws about 300 to 450 W while gaming: the headroom means it never runs flat out (louder, less efficient) and leaves room for a future graphics card.
Efficiency rating
80 PLUS Gold
How efficient it is (80 PLUS). At half load, a Gold unit loses about 10 % of the energy as heat, a Bronze unit about 15 %. Gold means quieter and less waste.
Modularity
Semi-modular
Fewer unused cables in the case than a non-modular PSU, for less money than a fully modular one.
Standard
ATX 3.1
A recent version of the standard: it copes better with the very short power spikes of modern graphics cards and provides the 12V-2x6 connector.
Connectors
24-pin · 2 × EPS 4+4 · 3 × PCIe 8 · SATA
Compare these with what the motherboard (24 + 8 pins), the graphics card (8 pins) and any SATA drives need.

A sense of scale

  • ~445 W To deliver 400 W to the components, a Gold unit draws about 445 W from the wall (45 W lost as heat); a Bronze unit about 470 W.
  • 62 A 750 W at 12 V is more than 60 amps: serious currents, hence the thick cables and the need for fully seated connectors.
  • ~380 kWh/year That's a PC drawing 350 W for 3 hours a day.

Compatibility

  • Wattage: (processor + graphics card + ~100 W) × 1.5 for headroom, or the graphics card maker's recommendation. The calculator in the checklist does the maths for you.
  • Connectors: enough 24-pin, 8-pin EPS and 8-pin PCIe (or 12V-2x6) connectors.
  • Case: ATX format and maximum PSU length.

Beginner mistakes

  • Mixing modular cables from two different power supplies. The pinout on the PSU side isn't standard, so you can destroy components. Only use the cables that came with your PSU.
  • Mixing up EPS (CPU) and PCIe (GPU). These two 8-pin plugs look alike but aren't wired the same way. They're labelled "CPU" and "PCIe". Never force one in.
  • Leaving the switch on the back at O for the first boot.
  • Buying a very cheap no-name PSU: it's the last part to cut corners on.

Part 7 of 8 · The extractor fan

Cooling (CPU cooler and fans)

A tower CPU cooler (12.5 × 7 × 15.5 cm) with 4 heat pipes, and a 120 mm case fan.

What it does

All the electricity the components use ends up as heat. The CPU cooler (a heatsink with a fan) draws heat away from the processor and passes it to the air; the case fans keep that air moving.

  1. Thermal paste fills the microscopic gaps between the processor's IHS and the cooler's base (pushing out the air, which conducts heat very poorly).
  2. Heat pipes (copper tubes) hold a little liquid that evaporates on the hot base, rises, then condenses in the fins, leaving its heat behind: a very efficient way to move heat.
  3. Aluminium fins give the air a large surface to pick up heat from.
  4. The fan pushes air through the fins, from the front of the case to the back.

Airflow through the case: three 120 mm fans at the front pull in cool air, and one fan at the back pushes out the warm air. The CPU cooler blows in the same direction. With slightly more air coming in than going out (positive pressure), dust mostly gets in through the filters.

The alternative: a 240 mm all-in-one liquid cooler (AIO) replaces the tower cooler with a radiator mounted on the case. It costs more and mainly makes sense for very power-hungry processors.

Think of it this way Cooling is the kitchen's extractor fan and windows: without them, the heat from the stoves makes the kitchen unbearable and the chef has to slow down.

How to read the spec sheet

Typical specs: a tower cooler + 120 mm fan

Height
155 mm
Compare with the case's "maximum CPU cooler height" (165 mm here).
Heat pipes
4 × 6 mm
How many there are, and how thick, gives you an idea of how much heat they can carry. 4 heat pipes are fine for mid-range processors.
Fan
120 mm, PWM, 500–1,800 RPM
The 4-pin PWM connector lets the motherboard adjust the speed to the temperature: quiet at idle, faster under load.
Noise / airflow
≤ 30 dB(A) · ~60 CFM
The maximum noise (in decibels) and the airflow (in CFM). These figures depend on how each maker measures them, so compare them with care.
Compatibility
AM5 / AM4 / LGA 1851 / LGA 1700
The sockets the included mounting kit supports.

A sense of scale

  • 25 turns/s A fan spinning at 1,500 RPM makes 25 turns every second.
  • ~50× Thermal paste conducts heat about 50 times less well than copper, so you want the thinnest possible layer, just enough to fill the gaps.
  • ~100 W of heat to get rid of when a processor is working flat out: as much as an old-fashioned incandescent bulb, but coming from chips only a few cm² in size.

Compatibility

  • Case: cooler height < maximum supported height.
  • Socket: a mounting kit for your socket in the box.
  • RAM: enough room under the fan for tall memory sticks.
  • Motherboard: enough fan headers (CPU_FAN + SYS_FAN), or else a splitter.

Beginner mistakes

  • Leaving the plastic film on the cooler's base. The all-time classic: the processor overheats within seconds.
  • Too much (or no) paste. A pea-sized blob in the centre is enough. If the base already has paste on it, don't add more.
  • Mounting a fan the wrong way round. Arrows on the frame show the airflow; air comes out on the side with the struts.
  • Plugging the CPU cooler into anything other than CPU_FAN: you'll get an error message at startup.
  • Tightening one side all the way, then the other. Tighten a little at a time, in a criss-cross pattern.

Part 8 of 8 · The building

The case

An ATX mid-tower case (45 × 22 × 48 cm) with a tempered glass side panel and a mesh front.

What it does

The case protects the components, organises the airflow, hides the cables and gives you the front ports (power button, USB, headphone jack). The most common type is the ATX mid-tower: the power supply sits at the bottom under a shroud, the motherboard is mounted on a tray, and there's a 2 to 3 cm gap behind that tray for routing cables (cable management).

On the tray, brass standoffs raise the motherboard so it never touches the metal. Rubber-lined openings (grommets) let cables pass from one side to the other.

Think of it this way The case is the building: the walls, the ventilation, the service ducts. A well-designed building makes work more pleasant… and maintenance easier.

How to read the spec sheet

Typical specs: an ATX mid-tower case

Supported boards
ATX (and smaller)
The largest motherboard size it takes.
Max GPU length
360 mm
The longest graphics card that fits. Our 28 cm card has plenty of room.
Max CPU cooler height
165 mm
The tallest CPU cooler it can take, limited by the width of the case and the glass panel.
Cooling
3 × 120 mm front + 1 × 120 mm rear included
The fans that come with it, and the free mounts (often 2 × 120/140 mm at the top). A mesh front (fine grille) lets air through far better than a solid one.
Front panel
2 × USB-A · 1 × USB-C · audio
The front USB-C port only works if the motherboard has the matching internal USB-C header.

A sense of scale

  • ~47 L The volume of a 45 × 22 × 48 cm mid-tower.
  • ~once a second In theory, the front fans replace all the air in the case about once a second (less in practice, because of the filters).

Compatibility

  • Motherboard: a supported size (ATX).
  • Graphics card: length < maximum length.
  • CPU cooler: height < maximum height.
  • Power supply: ATX format and maximum length.
  • Front panel: USB 3 and USB-C headers on the motherboard.

Beginner mistakes

  • Running every cable on the glass side: it blocks the airflow and makes the PC hard to maintain. Route them behind the tray.
  • Forgetting to connect the front panel (USB 3, USB-C, audio): the front ports stay dead.
  • Standing the PC on a carpet when the power supply draws air from underneath.
  • Leaving the protective film on the glass (no harm done, but a shame!).

Before you touch any parts

Safety first

Building a PC isn't dangerous for you as long as you follow a few simple rules. The real risk is mostly to the components: a discharge of static electricity you can't even feel can be enough to damage one.

Static electricity

Walking across a carpet or pulling off a jumper can charge your body up to several thousand volts. You only feel a discharge from around 3,000 V, but a much smaller one is enough to damage a chip. The damage doesn't always show straight away: a part can work fine, then fail weeks later.

  • Discharge yourself often: touch a bare, unpainted metal part of the case before picking up each component, and every so often while you work.
  • Pick your spot: a hard floor (tiles, wood) rather than carpet, and cotton clothes rather than wool or fleece.
  • Keep each part in its anti-static bag until you're ready to install it. Build the motherboard while it sits on its cardboard box.
  • Hold everything by the edges: don't touch the gold contacts, the chips or the socket pins.
  • Safest of all: an anti-static wrist strap (it costs very little), clipped to a metal part of the case.

Mains power and everything else

  • Unplug the power cable for the whole build, and every time you open the PC later on. Leave the power supply switch on O.
  • Never open the power supply. Its capacitors can hold a dangerous charge long after it's been unplugged. If it fails, you replace it; you don't repair it yourself.
  • Mind the sheet-metal edges inside the case: they can cut. Take your time, and thin gloves are an option.
  • Never force a connector. If it won't go in, it's the wrong way round or the wrong plug.
  • Before powering on, check that no screws or tools are left inside the case: a loose bit of metal can cause a short circuit.

You'll also find safety reminders at the delicate steps of the build.

Hands inside the PC

Step-by-step build

Eleven steps, in the easiest order for a first build. At each step, the part in question animates into place; you can turn the scene around and replay the animation.

Step 1 / 11

  1. Getting ready

    • Tools: a Phillips screwdriver (size PH2 fits nearly every screw), a torch, some Velcro straps or cable ties, and a small bowl for the screws.
    • Work surface: a clear, hard table, not carpet. The motherboard box will act as a stand.
    • Static electricity: an invisible discharge can damage a component. Touch a bare metal part of the case regularly to discharge yourself, and avoid woolly jumpers. An anti-static wrist strap clipped to the chassis is a bonus. Read the safety rules again if you need to.
    • Keep the motherboard manual within reach: it shows where every slot and header is.

    Don't plug anything into the mains until the build is finished.

  2. The processor in its socket

    • The motherboard stays outside the case, resting on its box: it's much easier this way.
    • Lift the metal lever, then open the retention frame.
    • Find the gold triangle in one corner of the processor and the triangle on the socket: they must be in the same corner.
    • Hold the processor by its edges and lower it in without forcing or sliding it: it should drop flat into place on its own.
    • Close the frame and push the lever down (some resistance is normal). The plastic cover pops off: keep it.

    Before taking the processor out of its box, touch the metal of the case to discharge yourself. Never touch the socket pins.

  3. RAM in slots A2 and B2

    • Open the latches on slots A2 and B2: the 2nd and 4th slots counting from the processor (check the manual).
    • Line up the notch on the stick with the key in the slot: it only goes in one way.
    • Press down firmly and straight, on both ends at once, until the latches click. It takes more force than you'd expect.

    Hold the sticks by their sides: don't touch the gold contacts or the chips.

  4. The M.2 SSD and its screw

    • Remove the small screw from the M.2_1 slot (the one closest to the processor). If there's an M.2 heatsink, take it off and peel the plastic film off its thermal pad.
    • Slide the SSD in at an angle (about 30°), gold contacts first, with the notch lined up with the key in the connector.
    • Press it down flat and screw it back in, without forcing.

    Hold the SSD by its edges, without touching the gold contacts. Keep an eye on that tiny screw: if it falls onto the board, it can cause a short circuit.

  5. Thermal paste, then the CPU cooler

    • If the base of the cooler already has paste on it (often a grey square), don't add any.
    • Otherwise, put a pea-sized blob of paste (4 to 5 mm) in the centre of the processor: the pressure of the cooler will spread it.
    • Peel the plastic film off the base of the cooler!
    • Fit the mounting hardware for your socket, then set the cooler down flat, with the fan facing so it blows towards the back of the case.
    • Tighten a little at a time, in a criss-cross pattern, until it stops, then plug its fan into the CPU_FAN header.

  6. Standoffs, then the motherboard goes into the case

    • Check that the standoffs on the tray line up with the 9 holes of an ATX board (they're often pre-installed). Never leave a standoff without a hole above it.
    • If the I/O shield isn't built into the board, clip it into the case first.
    • Lower the board in at an angle, rear ports first into the opening, then rest it on the standoffs.
    • Put in the 9 screws without forcing them, starting with the middle one, which acts as a guide.

    Tip: feed the 8-pin EPS cable through the top hole before you screw the board in. It's much easier that way.

  7. The power supply at the bottom of the case

    • Fan direction: facing down if the case has a filtered vent under the power supply (as it does here), facing up if not.
    • Plug in the modular cables you'll need first (here, the graphics card's 8-pin PCIe cable): it's easier outside the case.
    • Slide the power supply into its bay, switch set to O, and screw it in at the back (4 screws).

  8. The cables

    • 24-pin ATX: the biggest connector, on the right of the board. Push it in until it clicks (it takes a firm press).
    • 8-pin EPS (labelled "CPU"): top left, it powers the processor.
    • Front panel: the small wires (Power SW, Reset SW, Power LED +/−, HDD LED) go onto the F_PANEL header, following the diagram in the manual. The button wires have no polarity, but the LEDs do.
    • USB 3 (the blue 19-pin connector): it's keyed, so don't force it. Then the front USB-C and the audio (HD_AUDIO).
    • Fans: the CPU cooler goes on CPU_FAN, the case fans on SYS_FAN (or through a splitter).

  9. The graphics card in the PCIe x16 slot

    • Remove the metal slot covers at the back of the case, level with the x16 slot (2 here, depending on how thick the card is).
    • Push the card straight into the PCIe x16 slot until the latch clicks, with its bracket sitting in the gap left by the covers.
    • Screw the bracket to the case.
    • Plug in the power supply's 8-pin PCIe cable (not one labelled "CPU") until it clicks.

    Discharge yourself one last time before picking up the graphics card, and hold it by the bracket and the edges.

  10. Cable management

    • Route each cable behind the tray, through the grommet closest to where it's going.
    • Bundle the cables together with Velcro straps, without pulling them too tight, and leave a little slack near the connectors.
    • The goals: clear airflow, parts that are easy to reach later on… and a tidy interior behind the glass.

  11. The first boot

    • Plug the monitor into the graphics card, then the keyboard, the mouse and the power cable.
    • Flip the power supply switch to I and press the case's power button.
    • What's normal: the fans spin, and the diagnostic LEDs light up one after another, then go out. The first boot can take several minutes (DDR5 memory training), so be patient.
    • The BIOS/UEFI screen appears: on to the next section.

    Before plugging in the mains, check that no screws or tools are left in the case and that the side panel is back on.

    Nothing happens (no fans spinning)?
    • Is it plugged in, is the power strip on, and is the power supply switch on I?
    • Are the 24-pin and 8-pin EPS pushed in until they clicked?
    • Is the "Power SW" wire on the right F_PANEL pins? To test it, briefly touch those two pins with the tip of a screwdriver (that's what the button does).
    Fans spin, but no picture?
    • Is the monitor plugged into the graphics card, with the right input selected on the monitor?
    • Is the graphics card's PCIe cable properly plugged in?
    • Wait a few minutes: memory training can take a while.
    • Look at which diagnostic LED stays on: CPU (processor, EPS cable), DRAM (sticks not fully seated or in the wrong slots: try a single stick in A2), VGA (graphics card or its cable), BOOT (no operating system installed: that's normal before you install one).
    • Is the processor newer than the board? Update the BIOS with the BIOS Flashback button.

After the first boot

The BIOS / UEFI and installing the operating system

The BIOS (now replaced by UEFI, though people still say "BIOS" out of habit) is a small piece of software stored on a chip on the motherboard. Every time the PC starts, it checks the hardware (the POST), sets it up, then hands over to the operating system.

How to get into it

As soon as you power on, tap Del or F2 repeatedly, depending on the board. From Windows 11: Settings → System → Recovery → Advanced startup → Restart now, then Troubleshoot → Advanced options → UEFI Firmware Settings.

The 4 settings to change

  1. Check that everything is detected: the processor (and its temperature, around 30 to 45 °C at idle), 32 GB of memory (if you only see 16 GB, a stick isn't seated properly), the NVMe SSD, and the CPU_FAN fan spinning.
  2. Turn on the EXPO (AMD) or XMP (Intel) profile: the memory goes from its base speed (4,800 MT/s, for example) to its rated speed (6,000 MT/s). The next restart can take a while (it trains the memory again). If things become unstable, turn it back off.
  3. Set the boot order: to install the operating system, put the USB stick first. Even simpler: the boot menu (often F8, F11 or F12) lets you boot from the stick just once.
  4. Save and exit: F10, then confirm.

Try it in the simulator: turn on EXPO in the "Memory" tab, move the USB stick to the top in "Boot", then save with F10.

Installing the operating system from a USB stick

  1. Prepare the stick on another PC: for Windows 11, use Microsoft's official Media Creation Tool and a stick of at least 8 GB (it will be wiped). For Linux (Ubuntu, Fedora…), download the ISO image from the official website and write it to the stick with a tool such as balenaEtcher or Rufus.
  2. Boot from the stick using the boot order or the boot menu.
  3. Follow the installer and choose the NVMe SSD as the installation drive. Windows 11 requires the TPM 2.0 security chip and Secure Boot, both already turned on by default on recent boards.
  4. After installation: install the graphics card drivers (from the chip maker's website), the chipset drivers (from the motherboard or processor maker's website), then the system updates. Finally, put the SSD back at the top of the boot order if needed.

    Around the tower

    Peripherals and connectors

    The monitor, keyboard and mouse are what you look at and touch all day long: they matter just as much as what's inside the tower.

    The monitor

    Size
    The diagonal in inches (1" = 2.54 cm): 24" ≈ 61 cm, 27" ≈ 69 cm.
    Resolution
    The number of pixels: 1080p (1,920 × 1,080, "Full HD") or 1440p (2,560 × 1,440, "QHD"). The classic pairings: 24" at 1080p, 27" at 1440p (equally sharp, roughly 90 to 110 pixels per inch).
    Refresh rate
    The number of images shown per second, in hertz. At 144–165 Hz, everything feels much smoother than at 60 Hz, in games and on the desktop alike, as long as the graphics card can keep up.
    Panel
    IPS (accurate colours), VA (good contrast) or OLED (perfect blacks, pricier). Adaptive sync (FreeSync, G-SYNC Compatible) stops the picture from "tearing".

    The mechanical keyboard

    Each key has its own switch: a small spring-loaded mechanism, more precise and longer-lasting than a membrane. There are three families:

    • Linear (often "red"): a smooth, quiet press, popular for gaming.

    • Tactile ("brown"): you feel a small bump at the point where the key registers. A good all-rounder.

    • Clicky ("blue"): a bump plus an audible click. Satisfying… for you, much less so for anyone nearby.

    The colours are only a convention and vary between brands. The curves show force (vertical) against how far the key is pressed (horizontal). Size: full-size (with a number pad) or TKL (no number pad, more room for your mouse). Layouts vary by country: QWERTY in the UK and US, AZERTY in France.

    The mouse

    Sensor and DPI
    The optical sensor tracks movement, and DPI sets the sensitivity (800 to 1,600 suits most people).
    Connection
    Wired, or wireless with a small 2.4 GHz USB receiver (more responsive than Bluetooth).
    Grip
    The number one criterion: the shape has to fit your hand.

    The connectors

    • USB-A

      The classic rectangle that only goes in one way. Black or white inside: USB 2.0 (480 Mb/s); blue: USB 5 Gb/s; often red or teal: 10 Gb/s.

    • USB-C

      An oval plug that's reversible. Be careful: it's just the shape of the plug. Behind it you might find USB 2.0 or USB4 at 40 Gb/s, video (DisplayPort) or charging up to 240 W: check the spec sheet.

    • HDMI

      The trapezoid you'll find on TVs and consoles. HDMI 2.1 goes up to 48 Gb/s (enough for 4K at 120 Hz and beyond). Version 2.2 has been announced, but compatible devices aren't expected before 2027.

    • DisplayPort

      A rectangle with just one corner cut off, often with a locking clip. It's the standard for PC monitors: DP 1.4 (32.4 Gb/s) is enough for 1440p at 165 Hz, and DP 2.1 goes up to 80 Gb/s.

    • Thunderbolt

      Uses the USB-C plug, marked with a lightning-bolt logo: data, video and power down a single cable, at 40 Gb/s (Thunderbolt 4) or 80 Gb/s (Thunderbolt 5). Common on laptops; on a desktop, you need a compatible motherboard.

    Important reminder

    The monitor plugs into the graphics card (the outputs lined up along its bracket, lower down on the back of the tower), not the motherboard (the outputs near the USB ports, at the top). The motherboard's outputs only work with the processor's integrated graphics: plug in there and you'll get a black screen, or the graphics card won't be used.

    Finding your way around

    Glossary

    Every technical term on the site. In the text, words with a dotted underline show their definition when you hover over (or tap) them.

    12V-2x6
    A 16-pin power connector (12 large pins + 4 small ones) used by powerful graphics cards. It can deliver up to 600 W.
    80 PLUS
    An efficiency rating for power supplies: how much of the energy drawn from the wall actually reaches the components. Common levels: Bronze (~85 % at half load), Gold (~90 %), then Platinum and Titanium.
    AIO
    "All-in-one": a sealed, pre-filled liquid cooler. A pump on the processor sends liquid to a radiator with fans, mounted on the case (often 240 mm).
    ATX
    The standard that sets motherboard dimensions (30.5 × 24.4 cm for ATX), where their mounting holes go, and the power supply connectors. ATX 3.x power supplies cope better with graphics cards' power spikes.
    BIOS
    "Basic Input/Output System": the start-up software stored on the motherboard. Out of habit, the word now also refers to its successor, UEFI.
    BIOS Flashback
    A motherboard button that updates the BIOS from a USB stick, with no processor or memory installed.
    Boot menu
    A menu shown at power-on (often with F8, F11 or F12) that lets you pick which device to boot from, just this once.
    Bracket
    The metal plate at the end of an expansion card, screwed to the back of the case. On a graphics card, it carries the video outputs.
    Bus
    A set of connections that carries data between components: the motherboard's "roads".
    Cable management
    The art of tidying cables away (behind the tray, in neat bundles) for better airflow, easier maintenance and good looks.
    Cache
    A small, extremely fast memory built into the processor (levels L1, L2, L3) that keeps the most-used data close so it doesn't have to wait for RAM.
    CAS latency (CL)
    The number of cycles between asking RAM for a piece of data and getting it. Combine it with the speed: latency (ns) ≈ CL × 2,000 ÷ speed (MT/s).
    CFM
    "Cubic feet per minute": the unit for a fan's airflow (1 CFM ≈ 1.7 m³/h).
    Chipset
    A chip on the motherboard that runs some of the connections (USB, SATA, some M.2 and PCIe slots) and defines which range the board belongs to.
    Core
    A complete processing unit inside a processor, able to run a program independently of the others.
    CPU
    "Central Processing Unit": the processor.
    CPU cooler
    An air cooler (a heatsink with a fan) mounted on the processor.
    DDR5
    The 5th generation of DDR memory. Faster than DDR4 and not compatible with it (the notch is in a different place).
    Die
    The silicon chip itself, where the transistors are etched. A modern processor can hold several of them under its lid.
    DisplayPort
    The standard video connector for PC monitors, recognisable by its single cut-off corner.
    DPI
    "Dots per inch": a mouse's sensitivity, the number of points it detects per inch of movement.
    Driver
    Software that lets the operating system talk to a piece of hardware (graphics card, chipset, network…).
    Dual channel
    A mode where the processor accesses two memory sticks in parallel (one per channel), which doubles the bandwidth. They have to be in the right slots, usually A2 and B2.
    EPS
    An 8-pin power connector (often 4+4) just for the processor, in the top left of the motherboard. It's labelled "CPU" on the power supply side.
    EXPO / XMP
    Profiles stored on the memory stick by its maker (EXPO for AMD, XMP for Intel). Turning one on in the BIOS runs the RAM at its rated speed.
    GHz
    Gigahertz: one billion cycles per second.
    GPU
    "Graphics Processing Unit": the graphics card's chip, made up of thousands of processing units working in parallel.
    Grommet
    An opening in the tray, often lined with rubber, that lets cables pass behind the motherboard.
    HDD
    "Hard disk drive": a drive with magnetic platters and a moving read head. Lots of capacity for little money, but slow and sensitive to knocks.
    HDMI
    A trapezoid-shaped audio and video connector, standard on TVs and consoles.
    Header
    A group of pins on the motherboard for plugging in internal cables: fans, the case's front panel, USB, audio.
    Heat pipe
    A sealed copper tube holding a little liquid: it evaporates at the hot end and condenses at the cool end, carrying heat away very efficiently.
    Hertz (Hz)
    The unit of frequency: how many times per second. For a monitor, the number of images it shows each second.
    I/O
    "Input/Output". The ports on the back of the motherboard make up the I/O panel.
    iGPU
    Graphics built into the processor: enough for desktop work and troubleshooting, but not for demanding games.
    IHS
    "Integrated Heat Spreader": the processor's metal lid, which protects the chip and spreads its heat towards the cooler.
    IOPS
    Input/output operations per second: a measure of how fast an SSD handles small, random accesses.
    ISO
    An "image" file containing a whole installation disc, which you write to a USB stick to install an operating system.
    Latency
    The waiting time between a request and its answer: nanoseconds for RAM, microseconds for an SSD, milliseconds for a hard drive.
    LGA
    "Land Grid Array": a socket where the pins are on the motherboard and the processor only has flat pads (AMD AM5, Intel LGA 1851).
    M.2
    A compact format for expansion cards (SSDs, Wi-Fi…) that screw flat onto the motherboard. "2280" means 22 mm × 80 mm.
    Memory training
    The part of start-up where the motherboard tests and calibrates the memory. With DDR5, the first time can take several minutes.
    Modular
    Describes a power supply whose cables plug in as needed. Semi-modular: only the essential cables are fixed.
    MT/s
    Megatransfers per second: millions of data transfers per second. The unit for RAM speed (often wrongly written as MHz).
    NAND
    The type of flash memory in SSDs and USB sticks: it keeps data without power.
    Nanosecond (ns)
    A billionth of a second. 1 µs (microsecond) = 1,000 ns, 1 ms (millisecond) = 1,000,000 ns.
    NVMe
    "Non-Volatile Memory Express": a protocol that lets an SSD talk directly over PCIe lanes, much faster than SATA.
    Operating system (OS)
    The main software that runs the computer and launches programs: Windows, Linux, macOS…
    Panel (IPS, VA, OLED)
    The screen technology: IPS (accurate colours, good viewing angles), VA (good contrast), OLED (each pixel makes its own light, so blacks are perfect).
    PCB
    "Printed Circuit Board": an insulating board carrying copper traces and components.
    PCIe
    "PCI Express": the fast bus that links the processor to the graphics card, NVMe SSDs and so on. It comes in a number of lanes (x1, x4, x16), and each generation (3.0, 4.0, 5.0) doubles the speed per lane.
    Pixel
    The smallest dot in an image on screen. A 1080p screen has about 2 million of them.
    POST
    "Power-On Self-Test": the hardware self-check the BIOS runs every time the PC starts.
    Protections
    A power supply's safety circuits (over-voltage, over-current, short circuit, overheating…) that cut the power if something goes wrong.
    PSU
    "Power Supply Unit": the power supply.
    PWM
    "Pulse Width Modulation": a speed signal sent down a fan's 4th wire, so the motherboard can match the speed to the temperature.
    QVL
    "Qualified Vendor List": the list of components (mostly RAM kits) the motherboard maker has tested.
    RAM
    "Random Access Memory": fast, volatile working memory.
    Resolution
    The number of pixels in an image, as width × height: 2,560 × 1,440, for example.
    SATA
    The older interface used by 2.5" SSDs and hard drives, limited to about 550 MB/s. It needs a data cable and a power cable.
    Secure Boot
    A UEFI feature that only lets signed operating systems start, to block some malware at boot time.
    Slot
    A place where you plug in a component: a RAM slot, a PCIe slot, an M.2 slot.
    SMT
    "Simultaneous Multithreading" (Hyper-Threading at Intel): one core handles two threads at once so it's never left idle.
    Socket
    The processor's mount on the motherboard. The processor and the board must have exactly the same socket.
    SSD
    "Solid State Drive": storage built on flash memory, with no moving parts.
    Standoff
    A small post screwed into the case that the motherboard is mounted on, so the board never touches the metal.
    Static electricity (ESD)
    An electric charge your body builds up through friction (carpet, wool, fleece). Its sudden release ("electrostatic discharge") can damage a chip without you feeling a thing. To avoid it, touch the metal of the case, hold parts by their edges, or wear an anti-static wrist strap.
    Switch
    The mechanism under each key of a mechanical keyboard: linear, tactile or clicky.
    TBW
    "Terabytes Written": the total amount of data you can write to an SSD during its warranty.
    TDP
    "Thermal Design Power": roughly how much heat (in watts) a processor gives off, which the cooling has to remove.
    Thermal paste
    A heat-conducting paste applied between the processor and the cooler to push the air out of the microscopic gaps.
    Thread
    A thread of execution: a sequence of instructions run by a core.
    Throttling
    A component automatically slowing itself down when it gets too hot, to protect itself.
    Throughput
    The amount of data moved per second (MB/s, GB/s). Not to be confused with latency (the waiting time).
    Thunderbolt
    A very fast connection standard (40 to 80 Gb/s) that uses the USB-C plug: data, video and power.
    TKL
    "Tenkeyless": a keyboard without a number pad.
    TPM 2.0
    A security chip (or a feature built into the processor: fTPM at AMD, PTT at Intel) that stores encryption keys. Windows 11 requires it.
    UEFI
    "Unified Extensible Firmware Interface": the modern successor to the BIOS, with a graphical interface, mouse support and Secure Boot.
    USB / USB-C
    The universal connection standard. "USB-C" refers to the shape of the plug (reversible), not its speed.
    Volatile
    Loses its data without power, like RAM. Storage, on the other hand, is non-volatile.
    VRAM
    The graphics card's memory (GDDR6, GDDR7): very fast, it holds the textures and the frames being drawn.
    VRM
    "Voltage Regulator Module": the motherboard's power stage that turns 12 V into the processor's very low voltage (~1 V).
